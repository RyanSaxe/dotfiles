Bayesian vs gradient: two ways to learn from data

When you fit a model, you choose between an answer and a distribution. Gradient-based methods give you a single best-guess set of parameters. Bayesian inference gives you a probability distribution over them. The difference sounds philosophical until you have to ship something — at which point it becomes one of the most consequential decisions in the whole pipeline.

The shared setup

Both frames start from the same place: data $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, a model with parameters $\theta$, and a likelihood $p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$ that says how plausibly the model would have produced the data we observed.

From there, the two camps diverge on what they want from $\theta$. The gradient camp turns the problem into optimization — what's the single best $\theta$? The Bayesian camp turns it into inference — what does the data tell us about the distribution over $\theta$?

Gradient: find the best $\theta$

Maximum-likelihood estimation picks the $\theta$ that maximizes the log-likelihood of the data:

$$ \theta^* = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) $$

Stochastic gradient descent is the workhorse: take a small step against the gradient of the loss, repeat until convergence. The output is one parameter vector — a single point in parameter space. 

def sgd_step(theta, x, y, lr):
    grad = -log_likelihood_grad(theta, x, y)
    return theta - lr * grad
What you ship: a single set of weights, a forward pass at inference time, and $O(\text{model size})$ memory. This is why every frontier model, every production classifier, and every recsys ranker uses some flavor of this.

Bayesian: find the distribution over $\theta$

Bayes' rule turns the problem inside out. Given a prior $p(\theta)$ that encodes what you believed about the parameters before seeing data, the posterior is:

$$ p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \;=\; \frac{\,p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\,}{p(\mathcal{D})} $$

You don't get a single $\theta$ — you get a probability distribution that captures everything the data has told you about the parameters. To make a prediction, you sample $\theta$ from the posterior and average predictions across samples.

The catch: $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$ is rarely tractable in closed form for anything beyond toy cases. So you approximate. MCMC draws samples from the posterior over many iterations; variational inference fits a simpler distribution that's close to it.

What you ship: a sampler (or a fitted variational approximation), and an inference cost that scales with how many posterior samples you average over. An ensemble of 10 samples means every prediction is 10× the compute.

When to use which

Three quick heuristics that hold up most of the time:

Reach for gradient
  • Model is large (millions+ of parameters)
  • Data is abundant relative to parameters
  • A point estimate is acceptable downstream
  • Inference budget is tight
Reach for Bayesian
  • Calibrated uncertainty is the deliverable
  • Data is scarce; sensible priors exist
  • Model is small enough that posterior sampling is tractable
  • Decisions downstream reason about distributions

The hybrid landscape

Most production systems that "do Bayesian deep learning" don't run full posterior inference. They live in a hybrid space: MAP estimation gives a Bayesian-flavored point estimate. Deep ensembles train several models from different initializations and average them — sample-free uncertainty for the price of N forward passes. Laplace approximations fit a Gaussian around a trained model's weights. MC dropout reinterprets a regularizer as variational inference.1

The honest takeaway: gradient methods win on engineering practicality almost everywhere; Bayesian methods win when uncertainty itself is the deliverable; and the hybrid space is where most useful ML quietly lives.
