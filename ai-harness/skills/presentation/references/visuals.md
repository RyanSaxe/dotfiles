# Meaning-First Visuals

Use visuals to make the slide's idea faster to understand. A strong visual is not decoration, a component choice, or a way to fill empty space. It embodies the relationship the slide exists to explain.

## Start From Meaning

Before drawing, name the relationship:

- what changed
- what depends on what
- what is being compressed, expanded, compared, gated, or released
- what the audience should notice first
- what would take too long to explain in prose

Choose the visual form only after the relationship is clear. If the visual could be removed without changing comprehension, the slide is not done.

## Good Visuals Feel Inevitable

The visual should lean into the subject matter. A slide about a presentation skill can use slide/deck imagery. A slide about CLI tools can use an actual terminal/help surface. A slide about validation can use a gate, review loop, or release path.

The goal is not realism for its own sake. The goal is that the audience sees the visual and thinks, "of course that is the right way to show this."

## Avoid Block Confetti

Rectangles, cards, boxes, pills, and panels are useful only when they express grouping, state, sequence, comparison, or containment. Do not scatter labeled rectangles around a slide because the content has nouns.

Common failures:

- boxes that all have the same visual weight even when the ideas do not
- fake artifacts that look specific but do not reveal anything
- repeated card grids that make unrelated slides feel interchangeable
- visuals copied from examples without matching the new slide's meaning
- arrows between boxes that do not clarify a real transformation

## Use Examples For Taste, Not Structure

Open examples to study spacing, confidence, hierarchy, and craft. Do not treat them as components. If two decks reuse the same structural trick, it should be because the underlying relationship is the same, not because the example was nearby.

The single-slide examples are calibration. The user's content decides the slide.

## Review Questions

Before calling a visual done, ask:

- Does the visual show the relationship named by the slide title?
- Does one element clearly carry the first read?
- Are supporting elements visually subordinate?
- Are boxes or cards doing real grouping work?
- Would the slide still make sense if the presenter said nothing?
- Is this visual specific to this slide, or could it be pasted anywhere?
