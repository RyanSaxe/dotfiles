# Presentation Rubric

Use this rubric as a review lens, not a form to fill out. Do not score slides against it mechanically. A good deck is judged by whether the intended audience can skim it, understand the argument, and know what the presentation will make real in the room.

## Deck-Level Rubric

- **Purpose:** the deck has one clear reason to exist, stated in the user's language.
- **Audience fit:** density, tone, proof depth, and terminology match the room.
- **Thesis:** the main idea can be stated in one sentence before the slides are built.
- **Argument path:** the sequence moves the audience from current belief to intended belief or action.
- **Pacing:** major sections have enough space; hard turns get separators; proof does not crowd the main story.
- **Visual coherence:** typography, spacing, background, text color, and accent meaning feel like one deck.
- **Variety with discipline:** slides do not repeat the same layout unless repetition helps comprehension.
- **Pre-read clarity:** someone can scan the file and understand the gist without hearing the presenter.
- **Presenter leverage:** the slide leaves room for the speaker to add meaning instead of reading paragraphs aloud.

## Slide-Level Rubric

- **One job:** each slide has a specific communicative job, stated in context rather than selected from a fixed list.
- **Scan target:** the eye knows where to land first, second, and third.
- **One-line emphasis:** titles, KPI values, outcome statements, section labels, and callout headers are concise enough to render on one line in their available space.
- **Text roles:** large text carries the message; smaller text supplies context; nuance stays off the slide unless the room requires it.
- **Relationship-first visual:** the visual shows the relationship that matters in the content rather than decorating around it.
- **Message-visual fit:** the slide title and dominant visual tell the same story; if they point in different directions, redesign the visual or rename the claim.
- **Meaningful containers:** cards, panels, tables, and boxes group real peers or states; they are not generic buckets for excess text.
- **Balanced content mass:** the body feels composed on the artboard, not top-heavy, corner-heavy, or pinned to one dense strip.
- **Color discipline:** black text stays primary; accent color marks status, focus, comparison, or emphasis.
- **Specific but reusable:** the example is concrete enough to feel real while revealing a pattern the agent can adapt.
- **No explanatory scaffolding:** the slide itself should not describe why the layout works.

## Failing Signs

- The title wraps because the claim is too long.
- Most slides become three cards with different labels.
- The visual could be removed without changing comprehension.
- Text blocks explain relationships that spatial layout could show faster.
- Important information lives only in tiny labels, footers, or chrome.
- The deck looks centered at one viewport but drifts, clips, or scrolls at common review sizes.
