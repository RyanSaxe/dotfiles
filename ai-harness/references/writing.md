# Writing

Readers take a sentence's first noun as its subject and the next verb as its
action, and they go back and read it again when the words come in another
order (Bever, 1970; Frazier and Rayner, 1982). They read the first words of
each line and skip most of the rest (Nielsen, 2006). They read faster when
one item has the same structure as the item before it (Frazier and
colleagues, 1984), and they read meaning into any difference they see
(Mackinlay, 1986). Write so that readers understand each sentence the first
time through.

## Sentences

- Make the subject what the sentence is about, and put its verb right after
  it.
- Make the subject whoever does the action.
- Lead with what the reader needs, and explain why after.
- Give each sentence one idea.

## Words

- Use the plainest accurate word, and the standard technical term when one
  exists.
- Call each concept by one name.
- Give facts instead of adjectives.

## Structure

- Start each item with its distinguishing words, and give similar items
  the same structure.
- Use headings, code blocks, lists and diagrams wherever the reader needs
  them to find or follow something. Keep connected reasoning in a paragraph.
- State a rule on its own, without a list of cases after it.

## What to leave out

Leave out every sentence the reader can do without.

Last, check each sentence's subject and verb, and read the draft as someone
new to it. Rewrite any sentence whose subject isn't the one acting, and any
you had to read twice.
